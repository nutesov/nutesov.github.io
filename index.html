I am an applied economist. My fields are political economy, economic history, development and public economics, with a second line of work in industrial organization.

My research examines how asymmetric information shapes the taxation of farms and the adoption of new technology: in agriculture the farmer knows more about the harvest and its quality than the state that taxes the farm or the buyer who pays for the crop. Under autocracy, where most of the world's people now live, the state also collects its taxes and allocates new technology through local officials who know more than it does and whose interests may differ from the state's: a principal–agent problem inside the state.

Job market paper

Power Without Information: The Human Cost of Uniform Land Taxation in Subsistence Agriculture

Under autocracy the officials who collect the state's taxes answer to their superiors for targets, and the people they tax have little room to refuse. The paper measures what this cost in the Soviet countryside, where in 1933 the state taxed land rather than the harvest and left the collection to district officials answerable for targets. On the annual accounts of 272 collective farms of the Urals, which I digitized, the tax as collected cost one member in twenty-eight, in excess deaths and expulsions, against a benchmark in which the state sees each farm's harvest and takes only what the law required: 37 per cent from not seeing each farm's harvest, 63 per cent from the officials' extra.
