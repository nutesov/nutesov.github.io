Power Without Information: The Human Cost of Uniform Land Taxation in Subsistence Agriculture
PDF Abstract Appendix Draft, November 2026

A state that cannot verify the harvest it taxes at acceptable cost chooses between a crop share, which invites a reporting game between the farm and the assessor, and a fixed rent per hectare, which cannot be moved by a report of the harvest and cannot follow it: a fixed rent taxes uniformly farms whose harvests differ. In January 1933 the Soviet state replaced its claim on the assessed grain harvest of collective farms with a fixed rent per sown hectare, one rate for every farm of a district. On the accounts of 272 collective farms of the Urals for 1933 and 1934, I measure what uniform taxation cost in people and divide the cost between information and incentives. The sequence of the agricultural year fixes what each agent observed when it acted and hence the form of each claim. The rules estimated from the accounts, the district office's schedule, the farm's division of grain between seed and food, the seed relief and the response of population to food, convert any schedule of deliveries into members a year later. Relative to the same statutory total allocated on harvests with subsistence first, the policy as run cost 4,392 people, one member in twenty-eight: 707 from taxing uniformly farms whose harvests differed, 2,755 from the level of the wedge, the offices' collection above the norm, and 930 from its allocation across farms the office could not tell apart. Relief that came as seed in the spring rather than food in the autumn cost a further 964.

Working papers

Taxing What the State Could See: The Design of Soviet Grain Procurement and the Famine of 1933
PDF Abstract Draft, November 2026

A state that can verify its producers' harvest only at great cost taxes what it can see. Soviet grain procurement of 1932 was written on the sown area, on past deliveries and on a yield assessed from the standing crop, and it was collected by local officials who answered for a district's quota. This paper asks whether that tax, as it was actually collected, was enough to produce the famine of 1933. In the state's own post-harvest surveys of collective farms, recorded by region, the harvest of 1932 was in most regions enough to feed the farms' members; after the state's claims most of their farms fell below subsistence, and the depth of the shortfall predicts the rise in mortality between regions under every allocation of food within a region that the printed distributions admit. Ukraine, where the famine struck hardest, lost no more to the extraction than the other grain-producing regions, and the schedules make no reference to nationality. Within regions, districts with more Ukrainians had higher mortality, a difference the regional data cannot test; the archives point to the local campaigns of enforcement, the conditioning of relief and the restriction of movement, which no survey measures.

Victory over Peasantry: Extractive Institutions and the Big Push
Abstract with Vladimir Gimpelson and Paul Castañeda Dower  ·  Draft available on request

We examine how Big Push policies perform under the constraints of extractive institutions. Using novel micro-data from an All-Union audit of collective farms, we exploit the rollout of Machine Tractor Stations, the central pillar of Soviet agricultural policy, to estimate the impact of mechanization on collective farm performance, state procurement and peasant welfare in the early Soviet command economy. The Soviet system rapidly expanded technological adoption but failed to realize the full gains of increasing-returns technologies; adoption also did not raise living standards, leaving peasants on mechanized farms at or below subsistence levels.

Technology, Contracts, and Coordination: Modernizing Agricultural Supply Chains in Wisconsin Potatoes
PDF Abstract Figures and tables Draft, November 2026

This paper asks how much a quality-sorting technology and a long-term marketing contract with a retailer raise a wholesaler's price-cost margin. We study the Wisconsin fresh potato industry from 2010 to 2019, where a wholesaler invested in optical sorting systems (OSS) and then signed long-term marketing contracts with a modern retail chain. Employing a structural demand model and a difference-in-differences strategy, we find that the sorting technology increased price-cost margins by 4.6 cents per pound (about 10%), while marketing contracts contributed an additional 1.5 cent increase. The estimates may also reflect better inventory management and less spoilage, which we do not identify separately. Structured interviews with stakeholders in the industry indicate that the first investments were made under relational contracts and that formal long-term contracts followed once the returns were known.

Ongoing research

Repression and the collective farm in the Urals, 1930–1934 (data in progress)

The Soviet state relied on its local officials to tax, to mechanize and to repress, and repression is the subject of my ongoing research. From 1930 the Politburo set how many households each region was to remove as kulaks, and district officials chose which ones. Checking which households fit the state's definition took effort, so officials who answered for the number may have taken whoever was easiest to remove. I ask whom they removed, and whether repression helped the state collect grain, by frightening the villages that stayed, or hurt both collection and mechanization, by removing the most productive farmers. I have assembled a registry of about 80,000 people dispossessed or disenfranchised in the Urals, built from district archives and more complete than the widely used Memorial database, and matched it to the 1928 census of settlements; joined to the farm accounts and the stations' records, it will answer these questions. I am also extending the district records behind these papers across the Urals year by year, so that the state's taxes, tractors and removals can be studied together, district by district.
